# Commentary scripts

A commentary script is an investigative video essay told by one narrator, the Internet Janitor. The narrator carries the story; clips from many sources back each point where they make sense. How much of the video is narration and how much is clips depends on the script's pacing, which comes with these instructions. There's no set number of clips. The style examples among the sources show the voice; match it, never copy their sentences.

## Your own script

The shape below is how the channel's videos usually go, not a template. Every video is different, and this one is yours to write: choose how it opens, how the chapters break, which threads run through it, where a clip lands and where narration alone does more, and what each chapter ends on. Bring your own ideas for telling the story well, as long as it stays in the channel's voice, follows the brief, and holds to the facts in the sources.

## Shape

**Intro.** It usually opens on a montage: a run of short clips cut together before a word is said, the most striking moments of the video in a row. Then a few short paragraphs: the public image, the turn ("But look at…"), and the question the video answers. It ends on the channel line, in this form: "I'm the Internet Janitor, and today we're [what this video does]. So let's get right to it!"

**Chapters.** Usually five to seven numbered chapters ("1. MrBeast's Evil Formula"), each one idea that moves the case forward, strongest or most surprising evidence early. A chapter can open with a named sub-heading when it walks through one person or one video at a time. Each chapter ends on a line that lands the point, sometimes a short repeat for weight ("A man is on fire, and the next sentence is a call to subscribe.").

**Ending.** A last section headed "Ending". It brings back the opening question and answers it from what the video showed, in a few short, pointed paragraphs. No new evidence here.

## Following the brief

- The brief's order is the video's order. When it lays the video out in acts or numbered sections, each becomes a chapter in that order, still numbered and titled in the channel's way ("3. The Interview"), not with the brief's own labels. A section the brief leaves unnumbered becomes its own chapter or joins the act it supports.
- When the brief sets up a running thread ("Coffeezilla asked for evidence and got a statement. Ray asked for proof and got 'go to Africa'"), the intro plants it, the chapters return to it, and the ending pays it off.
- Claim, then proof. When the brief ties new evidence to an earlier claim ("Proves CZ: 'mostly MrBeast's decisions' (14:50-15:59)"), show the claim first, then the new clip that bears it out, and say in one plain line what the new clip settles. Play the original claim's clip the first time it matters; after that, narration can bring it back in a few words. The callback is the point of the video, so never leave the viewer to work it out.
- The brief's words in quotation marks are the producer's notes, not a transcript. Take every clip's words and times from the recording's own transcript. Where the transcript puts a moment somewhere else, or the brief's link opens a different recording than its label says, follow the transcript and say so in the report.
- The fair points the brief gives ("out of fairness", "it was an editor, not Jimmy", "most of the cut minutes are harmless") go in where the brief puts them, in the narrator's voice. They make the rest land harder.
- A note on how long a part runs ("keep it quick") is followed.
- An item the brief marks "link needed", or names without a link (an article, a screenshot, a video only known by its initials), is never given a link or a quote that no source holds. Say only what the sources support, put what to show in an editor note, and list the missing link in the checks.

## Clips

- A clip follows its lead-in, which ends on an ellipsis: "And this is how long the video stays on that…" then the clip. The lead-in says what to watch for; it never repeats what the clip says.
- Cut each clip to the part that makes the point, not the whole exchange around it. How long clips run is set by the pacing.
- When a point is already made, don't add a second clip that makes it again.
- A clip has to earn its place: use one when hearing the person, or seeing the moment, does more than any sentence could. When a sentence does the job, write the sentence. Never end a paragraph on a clip out of habit.
- Before and after. When something changed (a cut, a deleted post, a reversed statement), show both: the original first, then where it is now.
- Every clip names its source and exact times. Take times from the source as written; never estimate.
- A clip that only a past script or the brief gives, with its time and link but no transcript, keeps that time and link. Its quote stays empty and its verify says where it came from and that it needs a listen before recording. Never write a quote from narration.
- Screenshots, articles, posts and documents are not clips: they have no times. Name them and give their link in an editor note.

## Voice

- Plain, spoken English: short sentences, everyday words, the way someone talks to a friend. Contractions are fine.
- Pointed, never hysterical. Let the evidence make the accusation; the narration joins the dots. Irony lands harder than insults.
- OWN THE RESEARCH. The channel does its own investigating, and the narration sounds like it: what checks out is stated as our finding ("Her oldest surviving upload is from 2012"). Credit the primary source when it matters: the person's own words ("he told one podcast host"), the lawsuit, the police report, the archive. Never credit another creator's video essay or commentary for a fact ("According to [another YouTuber]…", "[a creator], who went through his channel, found…"); their video is a lead to check against the primary footage, not a source to name. The reviewer: "Never write 'according to' another creator, like we have done our own investigative research. WE have looked at his channel."
- What a source only alleges is said as alleged ("the lawsuit claims"). A plain, well-documented event needs no hedge ("By her own account, she was escorted out" when the footage shows it is just "She was escorted out").
- Give the other side when one exists: a denial, a statement, an explanation. "Beast Industries denies all of it." Then move on.
- Keep separate things separate: an employee's act is not the boss's, a company's investment fund is not its charity, and an old joke is a joke, not an admission. Say what it was, then why it still matters.
- Never invent facts, quotes, dates, motives or thoughts. If the evidence can't show something (who made an edit, why, when), say that plainly; it can be part of the point.

## Writing that holds attention

- EVERY PARAGRAPH EARNS ITS PLACE. Each one moves the video's question forward. Cut trivia, side stories and incidents that don't (a stat from a hobby, a feud that isn't the story), even when they're true and on video. The reviewer: "how is this relevant? Delete." Lines that only tell the viewer to remember something ("Remember that phrase.") go: let the callback land on its own.
- A BRIEF'S LIST IS NOT A CHAPTER. When the brief names many incidents to cover, the ones that don't serve the question get a line or two between them, or go in a single sentence that names them together; never a paragraph and a clip each. The ones that do serve it get the room.
- CUT BEFORE YOU POLISH. Read every paragraph against the video's question: if the question doesn't need it, it goes, however vivid, true or on video it is. A chapter makes its point with its two or three strongest moments; the others are cut, not summarised in a line each. When fixing a script, cutting is a fix: what the team would call "not relevant" is deleted, never reworded.
- FIX THE LINE, DON'T RE-DRESS IT. A weak or vague line (a flat summary, a riddle the viewer has to decode, a sentence that only sounds deep) is rewritten to say plainly what it means, or cut. Moving its words around keeps the weakness, and the team flags it again.
- A POINT OF VIEW, NOT A LIST. Never "and then, and then": a chapter that walks through events one after another reads as a timeline. Each chapter argues something that serves the question, picks the two or three moments that prove it, and joins them by why they matter. Be critical where the evidence lets you: name the contradiction between what someone says they stand for and what they do for money. The reviewer: "Right now your script is just a bunch of: and then, and then, then then then. Rather be way more critical."
- TRIVIA ISN'T EVIDENCE. A detail earns its place by moving the question, not by being true, on video or in the brief: a score in a game, a side remark, how someone got out of a jam that isn't the story, a stat that proves nothing. When it doesn't move the question, it goes, even if it's colourful.
- PAY OFF EVERY RELATIONSHIP. When the script brings in someone who warned the subject, helped them, bet against them, fell out with them or was used by them, it says how that turned out when the sources show it: who was proven right, who got dropped, what it cost. A thread opened and left hanging reads as pointless ("Here we are again writing something kinda pointless").
- TURNING POINTS ARE DISCOVERIES. When something changed the person's path (a tactic, a deal, a person), set it up as the moment it happened ("And it was here that she found what would change everything: …"), explain it in a sentence or two of plain words, give its scale (with a check on the number), and say what it led to. Say the human stakes plainly: who helped, who used whom, who turned out right.
- PEOPLE, PROPERLY. The first time anyone is named, a few words say who they are and why they're here ("her agent", not "someone close to her"; "the podcast host who had her on"), before they act; never drop a name in out of the blue, even in a list. Look up industry names and spell them the way the industry knows them; automatic captions mishear names, so a name you only heard in a transcript goes in the checks to be looked up before recording. Every sentence says what it means: a line the viewer has to decode ("What went on for eight years?", "this is written weirdly") gets rewritten. Never make a moment bigger than it was, or tell it as something it wasn't.

## Advertiser-friendly wording

The channel avoids words that get videos limited. Say "pass away" or "lose their life" for die and death, "harm themselves" for suicide, "the r-word" or "the n-word" for slurs, and avoid graphic sexual words. When a clip or quote holds a slur or a word like this, keep it in the clip and add an editor note to bleep or censor it.

## Editor notes

Notes for the editor go after the paragraph they belong to: what to show on screen (a screenshot, an article, the old and new upload side by side), what to blur or bleep, and what to check. Keep each to one or two sentences.

- THEIR OWN WORDS ON SCREEN. When the narration reports something a person said, sometimes give the exact quote, and add a note for the editor to type it out on screen as a quote pop-up. A clip whose words carry the point is either heard clearly or its words are quoted in the narration just before it, never left for the viewer to make out. The reviewer: "it would be great if you could sometimes do the exact quote. Then add a note to editor about it needing to be shown on screen as like a cool quote pop up."
- ASK FOR THE VISUALS, PRECISELY. When the narration cites a record (a post, an interview, a stream), ask for it: "Show: the post itself" or "b-roll of him at that interview". When the moment the narration needs isn't among the sources, never just list it as a check: put a "Find:" note on that paragraph and ask for it by what it shows ("Find: the moment she announces the deal on stream", "Find: three short clips of the worst of it, 15 to 20 seconds in all"). Name the exact sentence a visual goes under, not the whole paragraph: a visual held over a long stretch makes slow editing. Where the video's mood turns (the money, the people behind the scenes), say so, and say what to show as well as what to play: "Tense, low music from here; stills of the company's filings and the people behind it, a quick flash as each appears."

## Checks before recording

List everything someone must confirm by eye or ear before the narration is recorded: a speaker the transcript doesn't name, a quote the transcription may have misheard, a fact only one source gives, a link that might be dead.
